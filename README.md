## Overview

The Taos Hum is a low-frequency humming, droning, or rumbling sound reported by a small fraction of residents in and around Taos, New Mexico, beginning in the early 1990s. Hearers commonly describe a sound resembling a distant diesel engine that is most noticeable indoors and at night. Surveys in the early 1990s indicated that roughly 2% of the local population could hear it, with hearers reporting perceived frequencies in the range of about 32–80 Hz, modulated at approximately 0.5–2 Hz. In 1993, prompted by Taos residents and U.S. Representative Bill Richardson, a multi-institution team led by Joe Mullins (University of New Mexico) and including investigators from Sandia National Laboratories, Los Alamos National Laboratory, and Phillips Laboratory carried out a public investigation of the Hum. The team was unable to identify a single environmental acoustic source. The Taos Hum is one member of a wider class of regional 'Hum' phenomena, including the Bristol Hum (England), the Windsor Hum (Ontario/Michigan), the West Seattle Hum, and the Auckland Hum, which collectively remain unexplained.

> **Evidence**: Acoustic spectrograms; Resident questionnaire responses  
In response to a petition from Taos residents and the involvement of U.S. Representative Bill Richardson, an investigation team led by Joe Mullins (University of New Mexico) and supported by Sandia National Laboratories, Los Alamos National Laboratory, and Phillips Laboratory carried out acoustic measurements and a public questionnaire. ## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Spontaneous otoacoustic emissions / individual auditory sensitivity** _(Joe Mullins (1995 report context), Auditory researchers in subsequent literature)_

A widely cited explanation is that some hearers detect their own otoacoustic emissions or are unusually sensitive to ambient low-frequency sounds (e.g. distant infrastructure or geophysical noise) that are below the threshold of most listeners.

**Industrial / infrastructure low-frequency noise** _(Engineering acoustics community)_

Other regional Hum phenomena (notably the 2014 Windsor Hum) have been linked to industrial sources; analogous explanations are proposed but unverified for Taos.

### Skeptical

**Tinnitus / psychogenic explanation** _(Various otologists cited in popular reporting)_

Some commentators argue that the Taos Hum reflects spontaneous tinnitus or psychogenic perception in a sensitive subgroup, given the lack of an identified environmental source and the variation in reported frequency between hearers.
